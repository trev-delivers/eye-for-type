# Inclusive language

Copy that doesn't leave anyone out, talk down to them, or make
assumptions about who they are. Source tags in brackets point to
`sources.md`.

## Addressing people

- Talk to the person as "you". "The user" and "the player" feel distant
  [Apple-inclusion].
- Keep "we" and "our" for the product or company doing something real
  [Apple-inclusion].
- Ask for personal details only when the product needs them, and say why.

## Gender

- No "he", "she", "his or her" or "he/she" for an unknown person. Rewrite
  with "you", a plural, the role ("the owner"), or "they" [MS-bias]
  [Apple-inclusion].
- Gender-neutral nouns: "chair", "staff", "people", "workforce",
  "sales representative" [MS-bias].
- Avoiding gendered pronouns also helps translation into languages where
  the pronoun changes other words in the sentence [Apple-inclusion].
- If a form asks for gender or title, make it optional unless it's needed,
  and include options beyond 2. Use the person's own pronouns when writing
  about a real person [MS-bias].

## Disability

- UK and US conventions differ. GOV.UK uses "disabled people" (the social
  model: people are disabled by barriers), while Microsoft's guide puts
  people first ("people who are blind") [GDS-disability] [MS-bias]. Follow
  the product's market and voice guide; don't mix the 2 in one product.
- Either way, avoid words that suggest pity or illness: use "wheelchair
  user" over "wheelchair-bound" or "confined to a wheelchair", and "has
  [condition]" over "suffers from" or "victim of" [GDS-disability]
  [MS-bias].
- "Non-disabled", not "able-bodied"; "accessible parking", not "disabled
  parking"; "person with epilepsy", not "an epileptic" [GDS-disability].
- Don't mention a disability unless it's relevant [MS-bias].
- Avoid idioms that link impairment with something bad ("blind drunk",
  "turn a deaf ear", "crippling") [GDS-disability].

## Words with a history

- Prefer neutral technical terms: "primary" and "secondary" (or
  "subordinate") over "master" and "slave"; "allowlist" and "blocklist"
  over "whitelist" and "blacklist". Microsoft lists the first pair and
  treats militaristic and historically loaded terms as ones to avoid
  [MS-bias]. Check the product's term list for the exact replacements.
- Some everyday phrases have exclusionary origins, such as "grandfathered
  in" and "peanut gallery". Plain words are clearer anyway
  [Apple-inclusion].
- No slang that borrows from a culture, like "spirit animal" [MS-bias].

## Tone and assumptions

- An academic tone can make a product feel like it's only for highly
  educated people [Apple-inclusion]. Plain language includes more people
  (see `readability.md`).
- Humour is subjective and translates badly. It can confuse, annoy on
  repeat, or offend [Apple-inclusion]. Keep it out of errors, money,
  health, safety and anything about loss.
- Don't assume a family shape, a country, a calendar, a currency, or that
  someone has a surname, a postcode or a fixed address. Example names and
  places should vary [MS-bias].
- Don't call tasks easy or simple. If it isn't easy for this person, the
  copy has told them they're the problem.

## Checking

- Search the strings for he/she/his/her, guys, master, slave, whitelist,
  blacklist, sanity, crazy, insane, dumb, lame, cripple, blind to, suffer,
  victim, bound, simply, just, easy, obviously.
- Read each hit in context. Some are fine ("Sam's account: he").
